Making invariance-based causal discovery actually scalable

Dec 2024 · with Yifan Hu, Peter Bühlmann, Zijian Guo · arXiv:2412.11850

The problem

A company wants to know which ad channel causally drives sales \(Y\): clicks from search ads \(X_1\), or engagement from social ads \(X_2\)? A model fit purely to predict \(Y\) will happily use both, because both are correlated with sales — but correlation cannot tell apart the candidate mechanisms below, and spending against a non-causal metric wastes money. A powerful principle rescues us: if a set of variables truly causes \(Y\), the way \(Y\) depends on them stays invariant across environments — different time slots, regions, regimes — even as everything else shifts.

Invariant causal prediction turns this principle into a discovery method: search for the variable set whose predictive relationship is invariant across environments. The catch is combinatorial. With \(p\) candidate variables there are \(2^p\) subsets to test, so exhaustive search dies well before \(p\) reaches practical sizes.

From subset search to one continuous problem

Under a linear model, the causal coefficient \(\beta^\star\) is exactly the one whose squared prediction risk is equal across all environments \(e \in \mathcal E\). That gives a constrained program — minimize risk subject to risk-invariance:

\[ \min_{b\in\mathbb R^p}\ \mathbb E\big[(Y^e - b^\top X^e)^2\big] \quad\text{s.t.}\quad \mathbb E\big[(Y^e - b^\top X^e)^2\big]\ \text{is equal for all } e\in\mathcal E. \]

We turn this into a single unconstrained objective, Negative Weighted DRO (NegDRO), that minimizes the worst-case combination of environment risks — but where the combination weights are allowed to be negative:

\[ \widehat b \;\in\; \arg\min_{b\in\mathbb R^p}\ \max_{w\in\mathcal W}\ \sum_{e\in\mathcal E} w_e\; \mathbb E\big[(Y^e - b^\top X^e)^2\big], \qquad w\ \text{may be negative}. \]

Those negative weights are exactly what enforce risk invariance rather than merely penalizing large risks — but they also break convexity, turning the objective into a difference of convex functions. Unlike prior methods, NegDRO stays a continuous optimization: no subset enumeration, no integer programming.

Why nonconvexity isn't fatal here

Solving a nonconvex problem to global optimality is generally hopeless — but this one has special structure. We prove a benign landscape: under an identification condition stated directly in terms of the covariate distributions across environments, every (generalized) stationary point of NegDRO lies close to the true causal model \(\beta^\star\). So a plain gradient descent–ascent algorithm, which only ever finds stationary points, provably lands near \(\beta^\star\) — with non-asymptotic rates in both the sample size and the number of iterations, and runtime polynomial in \(p\) instead of exponential. Remarkably, the identification condition can be met with as few as two environments, versus the \(|\mathcal E| \ge p\) (or infinitely many) that earlier guarantees demanded.

Why it matters

This closes a gap between an elegant statistical principle and usable methodology. Invariance-based discovery had the right theory but an exponential price tag; recasting it as efficient nonconvex optimization makes it feasible at realistic dimensions, with the multi-environment data modern applications already generate — multiple hospitals, time periods, regional markets, or experimental regimes. To our knowledge this is the first causal-invariance method that reaches approximate global optimality for its nonconvex objective efficiently. If you liked the promise of invariant causal prediction but gave up on its runtime, this is the version you can run.
