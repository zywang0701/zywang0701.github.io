Learning models that hold up on unseen populations

The Annals of Statistics, 2025 · with Peter Bühlmann, Zijian Guo · arXiv:2309.02211

The problem

You have labeled data from several hospitals, or several regions, or several years, and you want a prediction model that works on a new population you haven't sampled yet. The default move — pool everything and minimize average error — quietly optimizes for the majority source and can perform badly exactly where the target differs most. What we want instead is a model whose performance degrades gracefully across any plausible target that looks like a mixture of the sources we've seen.

A reward, and its worst case

We score a model \(f\) on a distribution \(T\) by its reward — how much of the outcome's variance it explains relative to predicting nothing:

\[ R_T(f) \;=\; \mathbb E_{(X,Y)\sim T}\big[\, Y^2 - (Y - f(X))^2 \,\big]. \]

Then we build an uncertainty set \(\mathcal C(Q_X)\) of target distributions whose conditional \(Y\mid X\) is a mixture of the sources', and define the robust model as the one whose worst-case reward over that set is largest:

\[ f^\star \;=\; \arg\max_{f\in\mathcal F}\ \min_{T\in\mathcal C(Q_X)} R_T(f). \]

This reward-based formulation matters: unlike squared-error or regret losses, it stays computationally tractable under arbitrary convex prior information about the target and does not let a high-noise source distort the aggregation.

The clean characterization

The headline result (Theorem 2.1) is that this minimax model is nothing more exotic than a weighted average of the individual source models \(f^{(l)}\), with weights that solve a small convex quadratic program:

\[ f^\star \;=\; \sum_{l=1}^{L} q^\star_l\, f^{(l)}, \qquad q^\star \;=\; \arg\min_{q\in\Delta_L}\ q^\top \Gamma\, q, \]

where \(\Gamma\) is the Gram matrix of the source models. Geometrically, \(f^\star\) is simply the point in the convex hull of the source models that sits closest to the null model — a picture worth more than the formula:

Why the characterization is useful

Because \(f^\star\) is an aggregate of per-source models, you can fit each \(f^{(l)}\) with any machine-learning algorithm you like — random forests, gradient boosting, neural networks — and then combine them by solving the tiny QP. That also gives the method a federated-learning reading: sources never share raw data, only their fitted models, and the optimal weights \(q^\star\) reveal how important each source is for predicting a given target. Estimating those weights well is delicate, so we add a bias-corrected estimator that provably improves the convergence rate over the naive plug-in.

Why it matters

A model fit to pooled multi-source data optimizes for the population you happened to sample most. This framework instead produces a model that performs well for every mixture of the sources, computes efficiently through standard quadratic programming, respects privacy constraints, and comes with uncertainty quantification. It turns "will this model transfer?" from a hope into something you can optimize for directly.
